## Reasoning method extracted for KB-2

The source does not provide a measurement formula for value. Its usable
discipline is relational: first establish what the buyer would otherwise do;
then ask what difference the offer makes in that comparison. The KB-2 method
therefore makes five distinct claims rather than compressing them into a
benefit sentence:

1. **Baseline:** an evidenced buyer alternative in a named situation.
2. **Difference:** an evidenced capability or operating change versus that
   baseline.
3. **Mechanism:** a causal account of how the change alters work, risk,
   control, speed, or another intermediate condition.
4. **Outcome:** the consequence that matters to the buyer in that situation.
5. **Proof:** the evidence class that supports the preceding claim.

Only the first two may be established by M1 and product evidence alone. A
demo can establish the existence of a capability. It cannot establish that the
capability changes a buyer's work, that the change occurs in use, or that the
buyer values the result. That distinction is a domain rule inferred from the
different evidence burden of each claim, not a claim that Dunford measured a
specific outcome.

## Diagnostic questions

- Which current approach is evidenced for this buyer and buying situation?
- What specifically changes in that approach if the capability is used?
- Does the claimed difference describe an operating change, or merely a
  product attribute?
- What buyer outcome is directly reported, and what outcome is only a
  plausible inference?
- What would falsify the proposed chain?

## Worked application

An operations team presently reconciles a spreadsheet during a weekly meeting.
The product demonstration shows that approved ownership changes are shared
across two systems. The baseline and difference can be supported if each is
documented. It remains unproven that fewer assignments are lost, that meetings
become shorter, or that the team would switch. Those are separate hypotheses
to test with customer use or direct testimony. The valid strategic output is a
researchable candidate value, not an asserted outcome claim.

## Counterexamples and limits

**Feature comparison masquerading as value:** "we use AI while the spreadsheet
does not" names a product property but no buyer-relevant operating difference.

**Phantom-competitor value:** comparing a small product to an enterprise
platform because the contrast makes it look simpler, when buyers actually keep
using a manual workflow. The comparison is strategically attractive but
unsupported.

**Context overreach:** a suitable category can make an honest value legible;
it does not make the value true. Customer evidence remains the authority on
outcomes, switching, and perception.
